The carry-on has become my favorite systems-thinking object because it is honest
in a way software rarely is. If something is unnecessary, you feel it right
away. If something is badly placed, the friction compounds at every checkpoint,
every taxi, every hotel room, every rushed platform change.

That directness is useful. It turns preference into architecture. You stop
asking whether an item is nice to have and start asking whether it earns its
weight, its location, and its recovery plan when the day stops behaving.

## Design for repeated transitions

Travel exposes how much value lives in transitions rather than destinations. A
bag is touched when you leave home, check in, board, land, move through customs,
and reset in a new room. The same object has to perform under quiet preparation
and under time pressure.

That is exactly how reliable systems behave. They are not judged only by their
steady state. They are judged by handoffs, retries, degraded modes, and the
quality of the experience when the environment gets noisy.

When I repack after a trip, I usually write down three things:

1. What I reached for most often.
2. What never justified its footprint.
3. What failed because it had no obvious place to live.

That review loop maps cleanly into product work. It is the same discipline as
watching logs after launch or sitting with support transcripts after a release.
The useful question is rarely “Did this work once?” It is “Did this remain easy
to reason about under repetition?”

## Keep the kit legible

The most valuable travel upgrade is usually not a new object. It is legibility.
Pouches that explain themselves. Cables grouped by use, not by accident. A
notebook that always lives in the same sleeve. A pocket reserved for the single
item that matters when everything speeds up.

In software, legibility is the same gift. Clear ownership, obvious boundaries,
and enough structure that another person can step in without needing your whole
mental model narrated back to them. The system feels calmer because it is easier
to parse.

I keep returning to the same travel lesson: constraints are generous when they
force you to become more precise. A carry-on just happens to make the lesson
impossible to ignore.
